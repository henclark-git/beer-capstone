Beer, Industrialization, and Inequality

How a drink that kept ordinary people alive became an industry owned by a few.

Henry Clark

The argument

Beer follows one thread through history: a basic good that everyone needs slowly becomes a business that a few people own. It kept the poor alive, it marked the line between classes, and as it industrialized it gave millions cheap beer while a handful of firms captured the market.

  • Survival. Beer was safer than water — a shared benefit across classes.
  • Status. The same drink sorted people by what, where, and how much they drank.
  • Structure. Industry expanded supply but concentrated ownership and profit.

Act 1 — Survival

Beer was safer than the water

For most of history, the danger was not the beer — it was the water. Untreated wells and rivers carried cholera, dysentery, and typhoid. Brewing fixed that by accident: boiling the wort and fermenting it killed the bacteria, so a daily ration of beer was a safer way to stay hydrated than the local pump.

The clearest proof came in London in 1854. During a cholera outbreak around the Broad Street pump, the physician John Snow noticed that workers at the nearby brewery were untouched. They drank beer, not pump water — and the contrast was stark.

The point: before clean water, beer was public-health technology. Its first job was keeping people alive — rich and poor alike.

Act 2 — Status

The same drink drew the class line

Once beer was everywhere, it became a way to read a person's place in society. In industrial London, dark, filling porter was the working person's drink — it even took its name from the porters who hauled goods through the streets. The rich drank wine; laborers drank beer.

The split shows up in household budgets. Seebohm Rowntree's 1901 study of York set a strict poverty line and found that families at the very bottom could not afford any beer without going hungry. Just above them, beer was a large and steady part of working-class spending. For the wealthy, it was a rounding error — and not their drink of choice anyway.

The point: beer was the working class's drink. It was central to their budgets and culture, and largely absent from both the destitute and the elite — a marker of class in a glass.

Act 3 — Structure

Beer scaled up — and profit narrowed

From the 18th century on, brewing industrialized. Steam-powered London breweries, then giant American firms, brewed more beer per worker than ever. The U.S. is the clearest case because the data is so complete.

Here is the paradox. After Prohibition there were hundreds of brewers; by 1980 a wave of mergers left only about 40 large ones, and the top four firms' share of the market climbed from 16% in 1950 to 93% by 2009. Then the craft boom pushed the number of breweries past 9,000 — yet a few giants still sell the overwhelming majority of the beer.

The point: industrialization expanded supply — cheaper, more abundant beer — while concentrating ownership. More breweries than ever, but most of the beer (and the profit) flows through a handful of firms.

One thread, three acts

The same pint tells one story. Beer began as survival — safer than the water and good for everyone. It hardened into status — the drink that marked the working class. And when it industrialized, it became structure — more beer for more people, but profit funneled to fewer and fewer firms.

That is the throughline: as beer got safer, cheaper, and more abundant, the benefits spread outward to drinkers while ownership narrowed. A good that everyone needs became a business that a few people own — progress and inequality in the same glass.
